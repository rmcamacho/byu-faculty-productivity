# Timestamped Notes: “Becoming BYU in an Age of Artificial Intelligence”

**Speaker:** Elder Gerrit W. Gong
**Occasion:** BYU University Conference
**Date:** August 24, 2026
**Duration:** approximately 36 minutes
**Official source:** <https://speeches.byu.edu/talks/gerrit-w-gong/becoming-byu-in-an-age-of-artificial-intelligence/>

## Detailed Outline

### 0:00–2:38 — An invitation to become

Elder Gong begins by thanking BYU leaders, faculty, staff, and administrators. He frames the address around three hopes: that employees continue becoming joyful lifelong disciples of Jesus Christ, that they help students become educated and devoted lifelong disciples, and that the campus community help BYU fulfill its purpose in an age shaped by artificial intelligence.

**Course-design question:** How does this course help both students and faculty become, rather than merely complete tasks?

### 2:38–5:34 — Lifelong discipleship and pivotal student transitions

Examples of faithful BYU graduates illustrate that the effects of a BYU education extend far beyond assignments, laboratories, examinations, and graduation. Faculty and staff help students develop faith in Jesus Christ, prepare for family and relationships, and gain an excellent education for professional life. Employees foster lifelong learning, spiritual foundations, character, moral grounding, hard work, and intelligent work.

**Course-design question:** Which experiences in this course contribute to durable professional capability, character, and discipleship?

### 5:34–8:55 — Faculty influence and human relationships

Small, sincere interactions can have lasting influence. Elder Gong recalls faculty members, Church leaders, and university employees whose teaching, example, kindness, and personal attention affected him as a student. The central point is that human relationships remain at the core of the student experience.

**AI-design caution:** Automation should preserve or expand meaningful faculty–student and student–student relationships, not quietly remove them.

### 8:55–11:13 — Covenant belonging and the breadth of BYU’s mission

Elder Gong recognizes first-generation students, staff mentoring, student employment, research, internships, field experiences, and study abroad as parts of the BYU educational environment. Across programs and services, BYU seeks to build a covenant community, encourage courage and distinctiveness, support mission-inspired scholarship, and ensure that no one is left isolated.

**Course-design question:** Could an AI-supported activity increase belonging, mentoring, or access to high-impact learning rather than only increase efficiency?

### 11:13–14:27 — Why AI requires attention now

AI is influencing economics, society, national security, perceptions of truth, important relationships, work, and social organization. Elder Gong offers two propositions: AI will affect every course, discipline, degree, workplace, family, and community; and its arrival is a major opportunity for BYU and the Church Educational System. It provides an occasion to reexamine and reaffirm educational purposes, processes, means, and ends.

**Course-design question:** What should remain unchanged in this course, and what should be reimagined because AI is now part of the discipline and students’ future work?

### 14:27–15:30 — Four framing questions

The address organizes the AI challenge around four levels:

1. How should AI affect students and university employees, including literacy, policies, and preparation?
2. How should AI affect courses, disciplines, departments, and degrees?
3. How should AI affect university curriculum, pedagogy, assessment, graduation, and professional preparation?
4. How can BYU contribute to public understanding of human identity, dignity, and flourishing?

These four questions can serve as the organizing framework for the tutorial’s course-redesign exercise.

### 16:01–18:34 — AI literacy and balanced judgment

Students need preparation to live and thrive in an AI-influenced world. Elder Gong distinguishes mathematical systems from living human beings and discourages both fear of AI and exaggerated claims that it solves everything. A complete classroom ban may prevent students from learning to use an important technology, while uncritical adoption would also be inadequate. Course policies should promote ethical, productive use that strengthens critical thinking, problem solving, and creativity.

**Course-design question:** What AI literacy will graduates need, and where can students practice it under faculty guidance?

### 18:34–20:59 — Policies, responsibility, and productive struggle

Faculty should gain enough firsthand experience to speak credibly about AI and should provide a clear course policy. When AI can deepen learning, students may be invited to use it, but they should remain knowledgeable, responsible, and accountable. Use should strengthen reasoning and engagement with the real world rather than encourage dependency, dishonesty, or avoidance of the cognitive effort required for durable learning.

The address distinguishes completing an output from achieving a learning outcome. An artifact alone may conceal whether the student learned; authentic explanation, reasoning, recall, and voice still matter.

**Assessment question:** What evidence would show that the student—not merely an AI system—achieved the intended learning outcome?

### 21:01–23:53 — Courses, disciplines, careers, and agentic work

AI is reshaping methods and capabilities across engineering, science, computing, and other fields. Coordinated AI agents may perform substantial portions of work that previously required human knowledge and process expertise. This will alter labor markets while creating new possibilities. Humans will still need to envision, design, decide, set priorities, and implement. Students therefore need to learn to work both hard and intelligently while developing responsibility, accountability, integrity, and Christlike character.

**ECEN application:** Identify which professional tasks students should learn to perform unaided, which they should learn to perform with AI, and which require human judgment or accountability that must not be delegated.

### 23:55–26:14 — Curriculum, pedagogy, and assessment

At the institutional level, AI raises questions about curriculum, pedagogy, assessment, lifelong learning, career preparation, retention, graduation, faith, character, and resilience. When a final artifact is no longer reliable evidence of student learning, greater attention must be paid to the student’s developmental process. BYU’s eventual AI principles can draw on established Church principles while being tailored to educational circumstances.

**Assessment-design question:** How can a course make the learning journey visible through design reviews, oral explanations, laboratory demonstrations, version history, reflection, or iterative feedback?

### 26:14–29:50 — Human learning, moral grounding, and lived example

Elder Gong calls for renewed attention to foundational modes of reasoning and communication, mathematical and scientific knowledge, literature, art, scripture, and other sources of human experience. These sources contribute imagination, aspiration, courage, moral grounding, and moral judgment. Some knowledge and character cannot be reduced to words or rules; it must be demonstrated and learned through lived example. Faculty therefore remain essential as models of precision, generosity, compassion, discernment, faith, and judgment.

**Course-design question:** Which expert habits, ethical judgments, or forms of professional character must students observe and practice with people rather than merely receive as generated explanations?

### 29:51–31:26 — Four priorities for a BYU education in the AI age

The address identifies four related educational priorities:

1. Protect and promote human moral agency and the choice to do good, using AI to enhance individual work.
2. Champion the intrinsic and divine dignity, worth, and value of people.
3. Balance efficiency with individual opportunity.
4. Pursue human flourishing and the possibilities available to God’s children.

Students also need moral grounding and the ability to recognize both human values and their absence. They should understand that AI systems have limits and that important parts of their operation may not be transparent.

**Evaluation question:** Does a proposed use of AI strengthen agency, dignity, opportunity, and flourishing, or does it treat efficiency as the only goal?

### 31:30–35:23 — Divine identity and habits of great learners

BYU can contribute a distinctive understanding of embodied human identity, moral agency, growth through intentional choice, intelligence, truth, light, and divine potential. Knowing one’s identity as a child of God can support both excellence and humility. Elder Gong recalls five habits associated with great learners and extends them to great teachers: welcoming correction, keeping commitments, working hard, helping others, and expecting and overcoming resistance.

**Course-design question:** How could AI be used in a way that reinforces these five habits instead of weakening them?

### 35:24–36:20 — Closing purpose

The address concludes with confidence that BYU can approach the opportunity wisely and receive the Lord’s guidance. The purpose remains blessing students through their whole BYU experience and thereby blessing people around the world as BYU becomes what it is meant to be.

## Themes For The Tutorial Exercise

Ask participants to evaluate proposed uses of AI according to these themes:

- becoming, not merely task completion;
- human relationships and covenant belonging;
- balanced firsthand AI literacy;
- clear policies, responsibility, and accountability;
- productive struggle and durable learning;
- learning outcomes rather than polished outputs alone;
- professional preparation for human–AI collaboration;
- visible evidence of the student’s learning process;
- moral agency, dignity, opportunity, and human flourishing;
- excellence joined with humility, character, and service.
